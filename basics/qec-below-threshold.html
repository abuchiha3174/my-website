Quantum error correction below the surface code threshold

Authors: Google Quantum AI and Collaborators
Source: arXiv:2408.13687 — dated August 24, 2024

Summary (paraphrased, not verbatim) Quantum error correction only helps if the underlying hardware error rate sits below a critical threshold — above it, adding more physical qubits per logical qubit makes things worse, not better. This paper runs two real superconducting-qubit surface-code memories (distance-5 and distance-7) and shows, directly and measurably, that the hardware is finally operating below that threshold: logical error drops by a factor of 2.14 when the distance goes up by two, the distance-7 logical qubit outlives its best constituent physical qubit by 2.4×, and the whole thing is decoded live rather than after the fact. A separate stress test at much higher distance (29) then finds the next obstacle: rare, correlated error bursts hitting many qubits at once, roughly once an hour.

What a gate actually is (a rotation, not arithmetic)›

A gate spins the "arrow" to a new direction. It never adds or subtracts a number the way +,−,÷ would.

A qubit's state isn't a number — picture it as an arrow (like a clock hand) that can point anywhere between "0" (straight up) and "1" (straight down), or anywhere in between. A gate is an operation that rotates that arrow to a new direction. It's linear algebra (a matrix multiplying a vector), not arithmetic on the value 0 or 1.

Example: the Hadamard gate takes an arrow that starts pointing exactly at "0" and rotates it 90°, landing it exactly halfway between 0 and 1 — an equal superposition. No amount of +, −, or / on the number "0" could ever produce "half 0, half 1"; a rotation can, because the state was never really "just a number" to begin with.

entangling gates, revisited A two-qubit entangling gate (like CZ, used throughout this paper's circuit) is the same idea scaled up: it rotates the combined arrow of two qubits together, in a way that afterward can no longer be split back into "qubit A's arrow" and "qubit B's arrow" separately.
How many numbers does a superposition need?›

Each bar doubles: n qubits jointly need 2ⁿ numbers, not more numbers for one qubit alone.

A single qubit's superposition always takes exactly 2 numbers — "how much 0," "how much 1" — no matter how fine-grained the in-between state is. There are only two basis states to mix, so there are only ever two amounts to specify.

What actually grows is the joint description of many qubits together: 2 qubits need 2²=4 numbers (one for each combination 00, 01, 10, 11); 3 qubits need 2³=8; n qubits need 2ⁿ. That's exponential, not linear.

why this is the actual point of quantum computing This 2ⁿ joint space is why simulating a quantum computer on a classical one becomes hopeless past a few dozen qubits — by a few hundred qubits, 2ⁿ exceeds the number of atoms in the observable universe. Entangling gates are exactly what make those 2ⁿ numbers impossible to split back into separate per-qubit numbers — which is why entanglement, not superposition alone, is the resource quantum algorithms actually exploit.
Interference: how a quantum algorithm "knows" the right answer›

Both paths agree on |0⟩ (+½ and +½ add up) but disagree on |1⟩ (+½ and −½ cancel exactly) — real arithmetic, not a metaphor.

The obvious next assumption — "superposition explores 8 states at once, so it computes all 8 answers for free" — is the most common misconception about quantum computing, and it's wrong. The moment you measure, the superposition collapses to exactly one random outcome. Left alone, that's no better than guessing.

The actual power is interference. Amplitudes aren't just "how much" — they carry a sign (more generally, a complex phase), so two contributions to the same outcome can reinforce or cancel. A concrete, checkable example: apply the Hadamard gate to |0⟩ twice. Hadamard sends |0⟩→+½|0⟩+½|1⟩ but sends |1⟩→+½|0⟩−½|1⟩ (note the minus sign). Tracking both contributions to each final outcome:

final |0⟩: +½ + ½ = 1 (reinforced)
final |1⟩: +½ − ½ = 0 (cancelled, exactly)
Measuring now gives |0⟩ with 100% certainty — despite a genuine 50/50 blur in the middle of the computation. Nothing random survives; it was cancelled by the arithmetic, not luck.
so how do you "know" the right answer? You don't, in advance. You engineer the gate sequence so the wrong answers land in the "cancels to 0" row, like |1⟩ above, while the right answer lands in the "reinforces" row, like |0⟩. Whatever has nonzero probability left when you measure is the answer — not because it was found, but because everything else was forced to zero first. Designing that routing for a genuinely useful problem (this toy example just cancels back to the input) is the real research challenge — it's why only a handful of algorithms have a known quantum speedup, not everything.
Full worked example: factoring 21 with qubits, start to end›

Same 4-step pipeline as the portfolio example, now applied to a real checkable math problem: factoring.

Setup: find a factor of N=21, testing candidates 2,3,4,5 mapped to qubit patterns 00,01,10,11.

where's the "data"? Nowhere in the qubits. A qubit pattern like 01 only means "candidate #2 on our list" — it doesn't hold the number 3, and it doesn't hold 21 either. The value 21 and the checking rule ("does it divide evenly?") live entirely in the oracle circuit — wired in ahead of time, separately from the qubits.
1. superposition: (½, ½, ½, ½) for (2, 3, 4, 5)
2. oracle (21 mod 3 = 0, others ≠ 0): (½, −½, ½, ½)
3. interference (reflect about average ¼): (0, 1, 0, 0)
4. measure: "01" → candidate 3 → 3 is a factor of 21 ✓
Step 2 is built from real gates (X, CZ, X) targeting position 01, same sandwich trick as the portfolio example. Step 2's state is provably entangled — the same factoring check used before shows it can't be split back into two separate qubits, because "which candidate satisfies the rule" is a joint fact.
about "loops" 4 candidates with exactly 1 correct answer happens to resolve in exactly one oracle+interference round — a lucky special case of this size. For a bigger candidate list, you repeat the oracle+interference pair in a loop, roughly √(number of candidates) times, each round nudging the right answer's amplitude a little higher, until measuring it reliably works.

Classical comparison: checking 21 mod 2, mod 3, mod 4, mod 5 one at a time is a 4-step loop. Quantum: one oracle call tests all 4 simultaneously, then one interference step, then one measurement.

Physical qubit → logical qubit›

The classical version: repeat a bit 5 times and vote. Any single flip gets outvoted. Surface codes do the quantum-mechanical equivalent of this, in 2D.

A physical qubit is one real, physical piece of hardware — a superconducting circuit, an ion, anything that holds one quantum bit. It's fragile: heat, stray photons, and control imperfections randomly corrupt it.

A logical qubit is not a new kind of hardware — it's many physical qubits, wired together so that if a few of them get corrupted, the group as a whole can detect and fix it, without ever directly measuring (and thereby destroying) the quantum information being protected.

why this can't just be "measure and check" In classical computing you can freely read a bit to check it. In quantum computing, directly measuring a qubit collapses its state — you'd destroy the very information you're trying to protect. Surface codes are built around syndrome measurements specifically to get around this: they reveal where something went wrong without revealing what the encoded value actually is.
Code distance: how many errors it takes to fool the code›

More qubits per logical qubit buys a bigger margin — but that margin only pays off below the critical threshold.

Code distance (d) is, roughly, the minimum number of physical errors that would have to land simultaneously, in exactly the wrong pattern, before the code is fooled into an undetected wrong answer. Higher d means more errors are needed to slip through — a bigger safety margin.

Concretely: think of the two valid logical answers as 00000 and 11111. They differ in all 5 positions, so the distance is 5 — you'd need all 5 bits to flip together to silently jump from one valid answer to the other. Fewer flips just produce an obviously broken in-between pattern that gets caught.

Physical qubits per logical qubit is the price of that margin. A surface code's qubit count scales roughly as d² (it's a 2D grid, not a 1D line) — a distance-5 code needs on the order of 49 physical qubits, a distance-7 code around 101 (the exact number this paper uses).

Physical vs. logical error rate, and the critical threshold›

The single most important plot in the field: whether the curve bends up or down as d grows is decided entirely by which side of p_thr you're on.

Physical error rate (p) — how often one physical qubit or gate operation messes up. A raw hardware property.

Logical error rate (ε_d) — how often the whole error-corrected logical qubit ends up wrong, after the correction machinery has done its best.

the pivot the whole field balances on Critical threshold (p_thr): a break-even physical error rate.
  • p < p_thr (below threshold): increasing d makes ε_d go down, exponentially.
  • p > p_thr (above threshold): increasing d makes ε_d go up.

This is why "below threshold" is the paper's central claim, not a footnote. The relation is approximately ε_d ∝ (p / p_thr)^((d+1)/2), and Λ = ε_d / ε_{d+2} ≈ p_thr / p is the direct, measurable signature.

The surface code: a 2D grid of checks›

One stabilizer highlighted (blue box): 4 data qubits, checked by neighboring ancilla qubits, without ever reading the data qubits' actual value.

The surface code is a specific 2D layout for turning many physical qubits into one logical qubit. Picture a checkerboard grid: some qubits ("data qubits") hold the actual quantum information; interspersed "measurement" or "ancilla" qubits repeatedly run local checks called stabilizers against their neighbors.

Each stabilizer check reveals whether an error occurred nearby — without revealing (and thus destroying) what the data qubits actually encode. It's the leading candidate for superconducting hardware precisely because every check only needs nearest-neighbor connections — which is how these chips are physically wired.

Transmon hardware, and what a "memory" experiment tests›

A transmon is a superconducting circuit engineered so its two lowest energy levels act as a clean, addressable |0⟩/|1⟩ qubit.

A transmon is the physical-qubit hardware used here: a superconducting circuit (Josephson junctions + capacitors), cooled to near absolute zero, engineered so its two lowest energy levels behave like an artificial atom's 0 and 1.

A quantum memory experiment is the simplest possible QEC benchmark: encode a logical qubit's value, hold it through repeated error-correction cycles, then check whether the right value survived.

Comparing distance-5 and distance-7: Λ and "beyond break-even"›

ε₇ isn't just smaller — it's smaller by the exact factor Λ predicts. That match is the below-threshold proof, not an assumption.

Λ = ε_d / ε_{d+2} — how much the logical error rate improves when the code distance goes up by 2. The paper measures Λ = 2.14 ± 0.02 going from distance-5 to distance-7.

a separate, additional milestone "Beyond break-even": the logical qubit's information survives 2.4× longer than the single best physical qubit among its 101 constituents survives on its own, unprotected.

Syndrome measurements and the real-time decoder›

If decoding falls behind even once, the backlog only grows — this is why "real-time" is a genuine engineering hurdle.

A syndrome measurement is one round of the ancilla qubits' local checks. It doesn't reveal the data qubits' actual value — it reveals a checksum-like pattern of where an inconsistency showed up.

The decoder is the classical algorithm that takes the incoming stream of syndromes and infers the most likely underlying error pattern.

why "real-time" is the hard part The hardware keeps cycling every ~1.1 μs regardless of whether the classical decoder has kept up. Many earlier QEC demos decoded offline, after collecting all the data — sidestepping this timing problem entirely.
2. What they did The core experiment, at a glance.
+

Building a useful quantum computer requires stacking many physical qubits into logical qubits and making the logical qubit safer than any physical qubit alone — which only works below the critical threshold. Nobody had cleanly proven a real chip was on the right side of that line. That's the whole paper: prove it, unambiguously, on real hardware.

3. How they did it Step-by-step mechanics, the below-threshold proof, and the extra stress tests — expand each.
+
Running one experiment, step by step›
  1. Set the answer. Prepare all the data qubits together so that, as a group, they represent a single logical 0 or logical 1.
  2. Run the clock. Every 1.1 microseconds, the measure qubits check their neighbors and report a syndrome — without reading what the data qubits equal. Repeat up to a million times.
  3. Feed the checks to a decoder. Every syndrome streams to a classical program (a neural network, or a statistical "matching" algorithm — they tried both) that guesses what errors happened and tracks whether the logical answer has flipped.
  4. Open the box at the end. Measure the data qubits and compare the decoder's best guess against what was originally put in.
  5. Repeat ~100,000 times per data point, to get a reliable failure rate — this is ε_d, the logical error rate.
Proving "below threshold" — measuring Λ›

They ran the identical experiment at distance-5 and distance-7 and compared the two failure rates directly. Distance-7 failed less often, by a very specific factor (Λ = 2.14) — and that factor matching the theoretical prediction is the actual proof. Not "we think this works," but "we measured two numbers and they have the mathematically expected relationship."

Breaking things on purpose›
  • Injected fake errors at controlled strengths on data and measure qubits, to confirm the theory's predicted curve shape between physical error rate, code distance, and logical error rate.
  • Built a full error budget — a simulation of every known noise source (gate errors, measurement errors, "leakage" to an unused third energy level, correlated errors between neighboring qubits) — and checked how much of the measured error each piece explains. Result: mostly explained, ~20% left over.
  • Tested stability over 15 hours, repeating the same measurement to rule out drift or degradation during long runs.
Stress-testing with the repetition code — and finding the noise floor›

To hunt for extremely rare failure modes too small to see at distance-7, they ran a much simpler, cheaper 1D repetition code (the "00000 vs 11111" idea) pushed to distance-29, for 5.5 hours straight, 2×10¹⁰ total cycles.

what they found Roughly once an hour, a burst of errors hit ~30 neighboring qubits simultaneously — not random independent noise, but something physically disturbing a whole neighborhood at once (the leading suspect: a cosmic ray or radioactive decay depositing energy in the chip). This sets a hard floor (~10⁻¹⁰) that adding more qubits alone won't fix.
4. Why it matters Tying the pieces together.
+
  • Below threshold (Λ>1): proves the "more qubits = better" strategy actually works on real hardware, not just in theory.
  • Beyond break-even (2.4×): proves it's already a net win over the best unprotected physical qubit, not merely "trending the right way."
  • Real-time decoding: proves the classical side can keep up — a separate, practical requirement for ever running a real algorithm.
  • The repetition-code stress test: proves the core strategy works, but surfaces a new physical obstacle (correlated error bursts) that still needs solving before this scales to a full fault-tolerant computer.
bottom line The core physics of error correction is confirmed to work as promised — but there's a newly identified, real obstacle standing between here and a full-scale fault-tolerant quantum computer.
