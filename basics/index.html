1.2

From noisy physical qubits to one protected logical qubit

The classical version: repeat a bit 5 times and vote. Any single flip gets outvoted. Surface codes do the quantum-mechanical equivalent of this, in 2D.

A physical qubit is one real, physical piece of hardware — a superconducting circuit, an ion, anything that holds one quantum bit. It's fragile: heat, stray photons, and control imperfections randomly corrupt it.

A logical qubit is not a new kind of hardware — it's many physical qubits, wired together so that if a few of them get corrupted, the group as a whole can detect and fix it, without ever directly measuring (and thereby destroying) the quantum information being protected.

why this can't just be "measure and check" In classical computing you can freely read a bit to check it. In quantum computing, directly measuring a qubit collapses its state — you'd destroy the very information you're trying to protect. Surface codes are built around syndrome measurements (1.8) specifically to get around this: they reveal where something went wrong without revealing what the encoded value actually is.

The whole premise of quantum error correction: build a logical qubit whose effective error rate is far below the error rate of any one physical qubit inside it. Whether that premise actually pays off depends entirely on the critical threshold (1.4) below.

1.3

Code distance: how many errors it takes to fool the code

More qubits per logical qubit buys a bigger margin — but that margin only pays off below the critical threshold (1.4).

Code distance (d) is, roughly, the minimum number of physical errors that would have to land simultaneously, in exactly the wrong pattern, before the code is fooled into an undetected wrong answer. Higher d means more errors are needed to slip through — a bigger safety margin.

In the repetition-code analogy, encoding a bit across 5 copies is "distance 5"; across 3 copies is "distance 3." A surface code is the 2D, quantum-mechanical generalization of the same idea.

Physical qubits per logical qubit is the price of that margin. A surface code's qubit count scales roughly as d² — a distance-5 code needs on the order of 49 physical qubits, a distance-7 code around 101 (the exact number the paper uses). Bigger distance is safer, but proportionally more expensive in hardware.

1.4

Physical vs. logical error rate — and the critical threshold

The single most important plot in the field: whether the curve bends up or down as d grows is decided entirely by which side of p_thr you're on.

Physical error rate (p) — how often one physical qubit or gate operation messes up. A raw hardware property.

Logical error rate (ε_d) — how often the whole error-corrected logical qubit ends up wrong, after the correction machinery has done its best. This is the number that actually matters for running an algorithm.

the pivot the whole field balances on Critical threshold (p_thr): a break-even physical error rate.
  • p < p_thr (below threshold): increasing d makes ε_d go down, exponentially. More qubits genuinely help.
  • p > p_thr (above threshold): increasing d makes ε_d go up. Bigger codes just accumulate more failure-prone parts than they can correct for.

This is why "below threshold" is the paper's central claim, not a footnote — it's the dividing line between "this scales" and "this backfires." The relation is approximately ε_d ∝ (p / p_thr)^((d+1)/2), and the ratio Λ = ε_d / ε_{d+2} ≈ p_thr / p is the direct, measurable signature (1.7).

1.5

The surface code: a 2D grid of checks

One stabilizer highlighted (blue box): 4 data qubits, checked by neighboring ancilla qubits, without ever reading the data qubits' actual value.

The surface code is a specific 2D layout for turning many physical qubits into one logical qubit. Picture a checkerboard grid: some qubits ("data qubits") hold the actual quantum information; interspersed "measurement" or "ancilla" qubits repeatedly run local checks called stabilizers against their neighbors.

Each stabilizer check reveals whether an error occurred nearby — without revealing (and thus destroying) what the data qubits actually encode. It's called "surface" because the qubits sit on a 2D surface, and it's the leading candidate for superconducting hardware precisely because every check only needs nearest-neighbor connections — which is how these chips are physically wired.

1.6

Transmon hardware, and what a "memory" experiment tests

A transmon is a superconducting circuit engineered so its two lowest energy levels act as a clean, addressable |0⟩/|1⟩ qubit.

A transmon is the physical-qubit hardware used here: a superconducting circuit (built from Josephson junctions and capacitors), cooled to near absolute zero, engineered so its two lowest energy levels behave like an artificial atom's 0 and 1. It's the dominant qubit platform for Google and IBM. (Fluxonium and gatemon are sibling designs — same superconducting-circuit family, different engineering tradeoffs.)

A quantum memory experiment is the simplest possible QEC benchmark: encode a logical qubit's value, then just hold it, running repeated error-correction cycles over time, and check at the end whether the right value survived. No algorithm runs on it — it only tests whether the logical qubit can sit there and stay correct.

1.7

Comparing distance-5 and distance-7: Λ and "beyond break-even"

ε₇ isn't just smaller — it's smaller by the exact factor Λ predicts. That match is the below-threshold proof, not an assumption.

Λ = ε_d / ε_{d+2} — how much the logical error rate improves when the code distance goes up by 2. The paper measures Λ = 2.14 ± 0.02 going from distance-5 to distance-7: any Λ > 1 is direct, measured proof of operating below threshold (1.4) — this is the paper's central piece of evidence, not an assumption.

The distance-7 code (101 physical transmons) reaches 0.143% ± 0.003% error per cycle — the directly measured ε₇.

a separate, additional milestone "Beyond break-even": the logical qubit's information survives 2.4× longer than the single best physical qubit among its 101 constituents survives on its own, unprotected. This means the error correction is a net win, not merely "trending the right way" — for years, QEC demos made things worse, not better.

1.8

Syndrome measurements and the real-time decoder

If decoding falls behind even once, the backlog only grows — this is why "real-time" is a genuine engineering hurdle, separate from the below-threshold result itself.

A syndrome measurement is one round of the ancilla qubits' local checks (1.5). It doesn't reveal the data qubits' actual value — that would destroy the encoded state. It reveals a checksum-like pattern of where an inconsistency showed up: e.g. "these two neighboring checks disagree," which fingerprints which physical qubit likely errored.

The decoder is the classical algorithm that takes the incoming stream of syndromes and infers the most likely underlying error pattern, so it can be corrected or tracked.

why "real-time" is the hard part The hardware keeps cycling through rounds every ~1.1 μs regardless of whether the classical decoder has kept up. Many earlier QEC demos decoded offline, after collecting all the data — sidestepping this timing problem entirely. Doing it live, with 63 μs average latency sustained over up to a million cycles, is an added achievement layered on top of the below-threshold physics.
