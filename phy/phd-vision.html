Four directions this could grow into

Each is SOP-ready draft prose — edit into your own voice, don't paste verbatim.

Direction 1 · Generalization

Does a suppression strategy transfer across materials?

A natural next question is whether a suppression strategy learned on one material system transfers to another, or whether each material's distinct defect ensemble demands its own learned control strategy. Answering this would clarify whether machine-learned pulse control constitutes a general-purpose tool for defect mitigation or a per-material calibration procedure — a distinction with direct consequences for how such methods could be deployed at scale.

Direction 2 · Adaptive / online control

Can suppression track a drifting defect bath?

Because TLS parameters drift over time, a pulse sequence optimized once will not remain optimal indefinitely. A second direction extends this static optimization into an adaptive, online control loop that continuously re-estimates and adjusts the suppression pulse as the defect bath evolves — directly in the spirit of adaptive Hamiltonian-tracking approaches already pursued in this space.

Direction 3 · Bridging to real qubits

Does it survive contact with a real fabricated device?

A third direction addresses translation: BCTDS characterizes defect ensembles through a broadband waveguide, independent of full device fabrication. Whether a suppression strategy discovered in this platform meaningfully improves gate fidelity on an actual fabricated qubit coupled to the same class of defects is an open, and consequential, translational question.

Direction 4 · Diagnose-and-mitigate framework

One loop instead of two separate projects

A longer-term direction unifies characterization and control into a single framework — first diagnosing a defect ensemble's parameters, then using that diagnosis to inform targeted suppression — rather than treating characterization and mitigation as separate research threads.

Spoken identity pitch

For interviews, cold emails, or saying out loud before you write it down.

One-liner

I'm an ML engineer who did the work to actually understand the physics — I'm not throwing a black-box tool at their data, I'm building the computational layer these labs already rely on, backed by real understanding of the physical mechanism.

Quick intro (10–15 sec)

I build physically-grounded ML for quantum hardware — specifically, using ML to find pulse sequences that suppress decoherence caused by two-level-system defects in superconducting qubits.

Fuller version (~40 sec)

My background is machine learning, not physics. But the labs I'm targeting already do serious computational work themselves — Hamiltonian learning, adaptive parameter tracking, algorithmic error suppression. What they need isn't another physicist, it's someone who can own that computational layer and understand the physics well enough for it to be trustworthy, not a black box. So this year I did the work to close that gap — driven two-level-system dynamics, Lindblad master equations, dressed states — and I'm using it to build an ML pipeline that finds pulse sequences that destructively interfere with TLS defect dynamics, directly extending Fitzpatrick's BCTDS paper. I'm not trying to out-physics the physicists in the room. I'm bringing the computational half of this exact niche, with the physical grounding to make it count.
