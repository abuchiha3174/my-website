Robust Multi-Mode Superconducting Circuit Optimized for Quantum Information Processing

Authors: Pablo García-Azorín, Francisco A. Cárdenas-López, Gerhard B. P. Huber, Guillermo Romero, Max Werninghaus, Felix Motzoi, Stefan Filipp, Mikel Sanz

Source: arXiv:2407.18895v2 [quant-ph] (dated March 24, 2026)

ABSTRACT
Verbatim Multi-mode superconducting circuits offer a promising platform for engineering robust systems for quantum computation. Previous studies indicate that single-mode devices cannot be engineered to simultaneously exhibit resilience against multiple decoherence sources due to conflicting requirements. In contrast, multi-mode systems offer increased flexibility and have proven capable of overcoming these fundamental limitations. Here, we present a multi-mode device optimized for quantum information processing. It features an anharmonicity of a third of the qubit frequency and reduced energy dispersion caused by charge and magnetic flux fluctuations. It exhibits improvements over the fundamental errors limiting Transmon and Fluxonium coherence and control, achieving ratios between the total coherence time and the gate time T2/tg one order of magnitude larger than Transmon and two times larger than Fluxonium for microwave charge drives, assuming equal dielectric and inductive loss quality factors and limited drive strength. It furthermore demonstrates robustness against fabrication errors, a major limitation in many proposed multi-mode devices.
MEANING

What this actually says, in plain terms

"Multi-mode superconducting circuit" — a single-mode qubit (Transmon, Fluxonium) stores its quantum information in exactly one circuit degree of freedom: one island, one loop. This device uses four circuit nodes / three coupled modes instead. The point isn't complexity for its own sake: single-mode devices face a hard tradeoff, where the circuit changes that suppress charge noise tend to worsen flux-noise sensitivity and vice versa. More modes means more free design parameters, which opens room to suppress several decoherence channels at once instead of trading one off against another. The cost is a much harder optimization problem — this device's parameters were found with evolutionary search, not hand tuning.

"Anharmonicity of a third of the qubit frequency" — anharmonicity is how unevenly spaced the energy levels are. You want this large, because it's what lets a control pulse tuned to the |0⟩→|1⟩ transition avoid also driving |1⟩→|2⟩ (leakage out of the computational subspace). Here α = ω₃−2ω₁ ≈ 750 MHz against a qubit frequency ω₁₀ ≈ 2.5 GHz — 750/2500 ≈ 1/3.3, "a third."

"Reduced energy dispersion caused by charge and magnetic flux fluctuations" — energy dispersion is how much the qubit's transition frequency shifts when the external charge bias or magnetic flux drifts slightly. This is the direct mechanism behind dephasing: a stray fluctuation that shifts your frequency randomizes the qubit's accumulated phase relative to what you assumed. "Reduced dispersion" means the device sits at an operating point (φext = 0.5) where frequency is nearly flat against both charge and flux noise at once — the single-mode tradeoff above, actually resolved.

"T2/tg one order of magnitude larger than Transmon, two times larger than Fluxonium" — T2 is total coherence time (how long the qubit's phase information survives); tg is gate time (how long one logic operation takes). T2/tg is the number of gate operations you get before decoherence wins — the practical resource budget for computation, not just an abstract coherence number. Under a fair comparison (same assumed dielectric/inductive material loss quality factors, same realistic cap on drive power), this device gets roughly 10× more gates-before-decoherence than a Transmon and 2× more than Fluxonium.

"Robustness against fabrication errors" — real chips never hit their designed capacitance/inductance values exactly; lithography has tolerances. Many proposed multi-mode qubits are fine-tuned enough that small fabrication deviations wreck their protection. This design was optimized to keep working well even when the fabricated component values miss the design target — a practical advantage, not just a theoretical one.
