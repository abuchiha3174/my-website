Notation decoder

This is normal to find intimidating — physics notation is dense shorthand built up over a century, not a language anyone learns all at once. The good news: everything below is a small, closed set. The same dozen or so symbols get reused across every phase of this notebook; once you've decoded them here, you're not decoding them again, you're just recognizing them.

1. What's inside the kets — the state itself

The bar-and-angle-bracket, | ⟩, is called a "ket." Read |x⟩ simply as "the quantum state named x." It's a label, not an operation.

SymbolSay itPlain meaningWhere
|g⟩, |e⟩"ket g", "ket e"The two basis states: ground and excited. These are the two poles of the Bloch sphere. "Ground" means lowest-energy, not zero-energy — a defect sitting in |g⟩ has whatever baseline energy the system has at rest; it just isn't glowing (nothing lower to relax into, so no emission).1.1
|0⟩, |1⟩"ket zero", "ket one"Same idea, computer-science naming. |0⟩=|g⟩, |1⟩=|e⟩ — two names for the same two poles.0.1
|ψ⟩"ket psi"Generic placeholder name for "whatever state we're currently talking about."0.1, 1.1
α, β"alpha, beta"Complex numbers (amplitudes) that say how much |0⟩ and |1⟩ are mixed in. |α|² is literally the probability of measuring 0.0.1

2. The operators — things that do something to a state

Everything here is a 2×2 matrix. A ket is a column of two numbers; an operator is a machine that takes that column in and puts a (possibly different) column out.

SymbolSay itPlain meaningWhere
σz"sigma-z"Reads off which pole you're at: +1 for |e⟩, −1 for |g⟩.1.1, 1.2
σx"sigma-x"The flip operator — swaps |g⟩↔|e⟩. This is what a resonant drive does, repeatedly.1.1, 1.3
σy"sigma-y"The third Pauli matrix — a flip with an extra 90° phase twist. Built by your code alongside σx, σz, but doesn't get its own diagram until you need phase-sensitive drives.1.1 (code)
σ⁻, σ⁺"sigma minus/plus"One-way operators: σ⁻ de-excites (e→g) and annihilates |g⟩ outright; σ⁺ does the reverse. These are the "raising/lowering" operators that show up inside the RWA.1.1, 1.5
H"H", "the Hamiltonian"Not an operator you apply once — it's the single object that determines how the state evolves in time, full stop. Every simulation you run is "solve how H pushes the state around."everywhere

0.1

What is a qubit, actually?

A bit is a switch. A qubit is an arrow that can point anywhere — until you measure it, and it snaps to a pole.

A classical bit is always definitely 0 or definitely 1. A qubit is the same two-level object you just met on the Bloch sphere in Phase 1 — its state is |ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩, a genuine mixture of both, right up until you measure it.

measure → get 0 with probability |α|², get 1 with probability |β|² (|α|²+|β|² = 1)
This is the whole "spooky" part: before you measure, both outcomes are simultaneously encoded in the amplitudes α, β — including their relative phase, which has no classical analogue at all.

Put N qubits together and you don't get N independent coins — you get one joint state living in a 2^N-dimensional space, and that state can be entangled: correlations between qubits that can't be explained by any shared classical description. That joint space, and what you can do to it, is where all the computational power comes from — Section 0.2.

0.2

Why superposition is more than "trying everything at once"

Not "compute all 8 answers for free" — compute with all 8 amplitudes, then interfere them.

The popular explanation — "a quantum computer tries every answer at once" — is misleading. N qubits do hold 2N complex amplitudes simultaneously, but you can't just read all of them out; measurement collapses to one basis state, chosen randomly by those probabilities. Reading out the whole superposition would take exponentially many measurements — no free lunch there.

The actual mechanism is interference. A quantum algorithm is a sequence of unitary operations engineered so that amplitudes leading to wrong answers cancel (destructive interference) and amplitudes leading to the right answer reinforce (constructive interference) — so that when you finally measure, the right answer is overwhelmingly likely. Designing that interference pattern for a useful problem is genuinely hard, which is exactly why only a handful of quantum algorithms with proven speedups exist.

0.3

Applications — proven speedup vs. still emerging

Worth being precise about which of these are mathematically proven and which are hoped-for, since the hype and the reality diverge a lot in this field.

Quantum simulationproven advantage

Simulating molecules, catalysts, and materials. This was Feynman's original 1981 motivation — a classical computer's memory needed to track a quantum system's state grows exponentially with particle count; a quantum computer's doesn't. Most direct near-term use case, and the one closest to your own field (materials/quantum-device physics).

Cryptographyproven speedup

Shor's algorithm factors integers exponentially faster than any known classical method, which breaks RSA if a large enough fault-tolerant machine is ever built. It's why "post-quantum cryptography" is already being standardized today, well ahead of the hardware.

Unstructured searchproven, quadratic

Grover's algorithm — a quadratic (not exponential) speedup for searching an unsorted list. Real, but a much smaller win than Shor's, and often overstated in casual explanations.

Optimization & MLunproven at scale

Variational algorithms (QAOA and friends) are actively researched for optimization and machine learning, but no proven, robust quantum advantage over the best classical methods exists yet for practical problems. Treat claims here skeptically.

All of this runs on today's NISQ (noisy intermediate-scale quantum) hardware — tens to low thousands of physical qubits, no full error correction yet. The gap between "proven speedup on paper" and "outperforms a classical computer on something useful, today" is still large. That gap is exactly a coherence-time problem — which is where Section 0.5 connects back to your own work.

0.4

How a real qubit is built: the transmon

A capacitor makes it an oscillator; the Josephson junction makes it anharmonic — that's what lets you address only the bottom two rungs.

A bare LC circuit is a harmonic oscillator: evenly spaced energy levels, no way to isolate "the qubit" from the level above it — a pulse tuned to 0↔1 would just as happily drive 1↔2, 2↔3, etc. Useless as a qubit.

Swap the inductor for a Josephson junction (a nanometer-thin insulating barrier between two superconductors that lets Cooper pairs tunnel through) and the oscillator becomes nonlinear. The energy ladder is now anharmonic — the 0↔1 gap and the 1↔2 gap are slightly different frequencies. That's the whole trick: a microwave pulse tuned to exactly ω01 addresses only the bottom two levels, which you now get to call |0⟩ and |1⟩ and treat as your qubit.

The "transmon" (the dominant superconducting-qubit design today) is this circuit built with a large shunt capacitor, which suppresses sensitivity to stray charge noise at a small cost in anharmonicity. It is, in every physically meaningful sense, an artificial atom — which is exactly why the two-level-system math of Phase 1 applies to it directly.

important distinction for your work BCTDS itself does not route through a transmon — it's a broadband 3D waveguide built by the Fitzpatrick lab team you collaborate with (not your own dataset paper) that drives and reads out the TLS ensemble directly. The transmon material above matters for a different reason: it's how other groups study TLS defects — watching a single qubit's own T1/T2 shift as a proxy for nearby defects. Your broadband, ensemble-level approach is explicitly differentiated against that qubit-based method.
0.5

Where TLS defects come in — this is your thesis

The junction's tunnel barrier — and every other amorphous surface on the chip — is riddled with atomic-scale TLS defects sitting in the qubit's own electric field.

The Josephson junction's barrier isn't a perfect crystal — it's a thin amorphous oxide (so are the substrate interfaces and surface oxides elsewhere on the chip). Amorphous solids are disordered enough that small groups of atoms sit in asymmetric double-well potentials and can tunnel between the two configurations. Each one of those is, physically, its own tiny two-level quantum system — a TLS defect — with its own resonance frequency and its own electric dipole moment, embedded directly in the qubit's field.

When a TLS's frequency happens to sit near the qubit's, it couples to the qubit's oscillating field and does one of two things: exchanges energy with it (a direct loss channel — this eats your T1), or fluctuates slowly and jitters the qubit's frequency (this eats your T2, dephasing). Across today's leading platforms, TLS loss in amorphous dielectrics is a — often the — dominant limit on coherence time.

1.1

Two states, one sphere

Bloch sphere: poles are the energy eigenstates, any point on the surface is a valid state.

A TLS defect — like a qubit — lives in a 2-dimensional Hilbert space spanned by |g⟩ (ground) and |e⟩ (excited). Any state is a point on the surface of the Bloch sphere: the poles are the energy eigenstates, the equator is an equal superposition, and the phase of the superposition is the state's longitude.

Your code builds this space with Pauli operators. Three of the four you'll use most:

σx =

σz reads off which pole you're at (+1 = |e⟩, −1 = |g⟩). σx flips between poles. σ⁻ is the "de-excite" operator: it sends |e⟩→|g⟩ and kills |g⟩ outright — that asymmetry is why it shows up in decay (Phase 2), not here.

1.2

The bare Hamiltonian — H = ω σz / 2

The two rungs of the ladder, split by ħω. A resonant drive (ω_d ≈ ω) connects them.

With no drive and no decay, the only physics is the energy splitting between |g⟩ and |e⟩. That's all H = ω σz/2 says: |e⟩ sits at energy +ω/2, |g⟩ at −ω/2 (units where ħ=1, which your code uses throughout).

Left alone, a state just accumulates phase between the poles — it doesn't move on the Bloch sphere in any way you'd notice in population. The interesting dynamics start the moment you add a drive term, which is Section 1.3.

in your code H_tls = Σ ωⱼ σzⱼ/2 — for N=1 this is just one term. init_freqs[0] is ω for TLS 0.
in your paper Figure 3's legend symbol ω_i ("TLS resonance frequency") is exactly this ω. Table 1's "TLS resonance frequencies" column is a list of these, one per defect.
1.3

Driving it: A·cos(ω_d t)·σx

A real drive is two counter-rotating pieces. One nearly freezes near resonance; the other spins fast and is what the RWA drops (Section 1.5).

To move the state off the poles, you apply a classical microwave field along x: A·cos(ω_d t)·σx. Because cos is a sum of two counter-rotating phasors, this single real drive is secretly two rotating terms — one that (near resonance) rotates slowly with the state and does almost all the work, and one that spins at roughly twice the frequency and mostly averages out.

This split is the seed of everything in Section 1.5 (the RWA). For now, the important physical picture: a resonant drive along x rotates the Bloch vector around the x-axis, sweeping it from pole to pole and back — that sweep is the Rabi oscillation.

in your code d_coeff() implements amp·cos(freq·t) multiplying Sx — a genuine lab-frame drive. Your simulation never makes the RWA; it integrates the full counter-rotating physics, which is exactly why strong-drive figures (6/7/10) show real Bloch–Siegert shifts that a naive RWA model would miss.

Rabi oscillations — predict, then check

Before touching the slider: on resonance (δ=0), guess the period. Then guess what happens to the peak height as you detune. Move the slider only after you've guessed.

generalized Rabi freq Ωgen = √(Ω²+δ²) = 1.00 Ω peak population = Ω²/(Ω²+δ²) = 1.00
P_e(t) = [ Ω² / (Ω² + δ²) ] · sin²( √(Ω²+δ²) · t / 2 )
δ = 0 (resonant) collapses this to the textbook P_e(t) = sin²(Ωt/2): full flops, period 2π/Ω. Off resonance the state never reaches |e⟩ — the drive is "fighting" the detuning.

1.5

The rotating-wave approximation

Keep the near-static term, drop the one spinning at ~2ω. That's the whole approximation.

Write the drive in the interaction picture (i.e. factor out the free ω σz/2 evolution). Four terms fall out of cos(ω_d t)·σx; two rotate at the slow difference frequency δ=ω_d−ω, two rotate at the fast sum frequency ω+ω_d:

H_I(t) = (A/2)[ σ⁺e^{i(ω+ω_d)t} + σ⁺e^{i(ω−ω_d)t} + σ⁻e^{−i(ω−ω_d)t} + σ⁻e^{−i(ω+ω_d)t} ]
near resonance (ω≈ω_d): the middle two terms are quasi-static — keep them. The outer two spin at ~2ω — average to ~0 over a Rabi period — drop them.
H_RWA(t) = (A/2)[ σ⁺e^{−iδt} + σ⁻e^{iδt} ]

This is exactly the "generalized Rabi frequency" object driving the plot above. It's an approximation — valid when A ≪ ω, ω_d. Push the drive amplitude hard enough and the dropped terms stop averaging away: that's the Bloch–Siegert shift, and it's real physics your lab-frame simulation captures but this formula does not.
