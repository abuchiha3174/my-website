1.1

Why quantum computers aren't just "free parallelism"

Light through two slits doesn't just make two bright patches — amplitudes from both paths add (bright bands) or cancel (dark bands). Quantum algorithms exploit this same effect on qubit amplitudes.

A classical computer's state is always a definite string of 0s and 1s. With 3 bits, you can represent exactly one number between 0 and 7 at a time — one out of eight possibilities, full stop.

A qubit can be in a superposition: simultaneously "in" |0⟩ and |1⟩ at once (see the notebook's own 0.1 for the ground-up definition if this is new). Put 3 qubits in an equal superposition and the system is, in a real mathematical sense, in a superposition of all eight states 000 through 111 simultaneously — this is exactly why some quantum algorithms are exponentially faster than any known classical algorithm.

the catch — this is not free You cannot simply "read off" all eight answers. The moment you measure a superposition, it collapses to just one of the possible outcomes, chosen randomly according to probabilities set by the state. Compute on all states at once, but you only ever get to read out one. Naively, that sounds like it defeats the whole point.

The way around this is interference — the same phenomenon behind the double-slit pattern on the left. Each possible outcome in a superposition has an amplitude, and amplitudes can be positive or negative (more precisely, complex). A well-designed quantum algorithm arranges things so that amplitudes leading to wrong answers cancel out (destructive interference) while the amplitude for the right answer reinforces (constructive interference) — so that when you finally measure, the right answer dominates. Designing that cancellation pattern for a useful problem is genuinely hard, which is why "find a good quantum algorithm" is still an active, difficult research area, not a solved problem.

1.2

Dirac notation: kets, bras, and the two products

This is the bookkeeping language every quantum-computing paper is written in — worth being completely comfortable with it before anything else. Take two column vectors of two complex numbers each, call them A = (A₁, A₂) and B = (B₁, B₂).

The ket — just a column vector

|A⟩ =
A₁A₂

A "ket," written |A⟩, is nothing more than a column vector. The angle bracket is just notation for "this is a vector, look at it as one object."

The bra — complex-conjugate, then transpose

A "bra," written ⟨B|, takes the ket |B⟩ and does two things to it: complex-conjugates every entry, and transposes it into a row.

if B = C + Di then B* = C − Di
The complex conjugate just flips the sign on the imaginary part. C and D here are ordinary real numbers; i is the imaginary unit (i² = −1). This is the entire operation — nothing more exotic than a sign flip on one part of the number.
⟨B| =
B₁*B₂*

Column became row, and each entry got starred (conjugated). That's the full recipe: bra = conjugate-transpose of the matching ket.

Bra × ket = inner product = a single number

Put the bra first, ket second: ⟨B|A⟩. Multiplying a row vector by a column vector gives a single number — multiply matching entries and add:

⟨B|A⟩ = B₁*A₁ + B₂*A₂ ← one complex number
This is the same "row times column" rule that gives a scalar when you multiply a row vector by a column vector in ordinary linear algebra — it's literally the dot product, just with a complex-conjugate baked into one side. Note ⟨B|A⟩ = (⟨A|B⟩)* — swapping the order gives the complex conjugate of the original answer, not the same number in general.

Ket × bra = outer product = a full matrix

Reverse the order — ket first, bra second: |A⟩⟨B| (that's a ket and a bra sitting next to each other, not a multiplication sign, even though it can look like one at small font sizes). A column vector times a row vector gives a full matrix — every entry of the column times every entry of the row:

|A⟩⟨B| =
A₁B₁*A₁B₂*A₂B₁*A₂B₂*
the one thing worth memorizing Bra-ket = scalar (inner product). Ket-bra = matrix (outer product). Same two vectors, opposite order, completely different kind of object — not just a different value. If a quantum-density-matrix formula ever looks like ρ = |ψ⟩⟨ψ|, this is exactly why it's a matrix and not a number.
1.3

The computational basis: |0⟩ and |1⟩

|0⟩ =
10
|1⟩ =
01

The two reference states every other qubit state is built from.

By convention (not a law of physics — just a universally shared choice), the state |0⟩ is the column vector (1,0), and |1⟩ is (0,1).

⟨0|1⟩ = (1)(0) + (0)(1) = 0
An inner product of zero means the two states are orthogonal — as different from each other as two states can be. This is the direct check, not an assumption.
why we normalize Every quantum state used in practice is normalized: ⟨ψ|ψ⟩ = 1 for any state ψ. This is a convention (physically, scaling a state by a constant doesn't change what it represents), but it's the convention that makes the probability rule below come out clean, with no extra division step. For the equal superposition of 0 and 1, that means a factor of 1/√2 is required on each term:
|ψ⟩ = (1/√2)|0⟩ + (1/√2)|1⟩
Check: ⟨ψ|ψ⟩ = (1/√2)² + (1/√2)² = 1/2 + 1/2 = 1. ✓
1.4

Measurement: projecting onto a basis

This lecture restricts to projective measurements onto pairs of orthogonal states — the kind actually used in practice, and the kind that will collapse the state you measure into exactly one of the two basis states.

There are infinitely many possible orthogonal-pair bases to measure in, but three show up constantly, each tied to one of the three Pauli operators (σz, σx, σy — the same three matrices from the notebook's own 1.1). Each named basis is that operator's pair of eigenstates — the two states that operator "sees cleanly," without mixing.

Basis nameStatesOperatorDefinition
Z basis|0⟩, |1⟩σzthe computational basis itself
X basis|+⟩, |−⟩σx|+⟩=(1/√2)(|0⟩+|1⟩), |−⟩=(1/√2)(|0⟩−|1⟩)
Y basis|+i⟩, |−i⟩σy|+i⟩=(1/√2)(|0⟩+i|1⟩), |−i⟩=(1/√2)(|0⟩−i|1⟩)

Both |+⟩/|−⟩ and |+i⟩/|−i⟩ are still 50/50 superpositions of |0⟩ and |1⟩ — what changes between them is only the relative phase, a +1, −1, +i, or −i multiplying the |1⟩ term. Different relative phase, orthogonal state, different physical outcome under measurement — this is exactly the amplitude-sign mechanism that makes interference (1.1) work. A quick check confirms ⟨+|−⟩ = 0 and ⟨+i|−i⟩ = 0 — each pair is genuinely orthogonal, same test as ⟨0|1⟩ above.

1.5

The Born rule — turning a state into a probability

Given a state |ψ⟩ and a measurement onto some basis state |x⟩, the probability of collapsing to |x⟩ is:

P(x) = |⟨x|ψ⟩|²
Take the inner product of the target state with ψ (a complex number in general), take its absolute value, then square it. Thanks to normalization, no extra division step is needed, and all the probabilities for a given measurement automatically add up to 1.

Worked example 1 — Z measurement

Let |ψ⟩ = (1/√3)|0⟩ + √(2/3)|1⟩, and measure in the Z basis (|0⟩, |1⟩).

P(0) = |⟨0|ψ⟩|² = |(1/√3)⟨0|0⟩ + √(2/3)⟨0|1⟩|²
= |(1/√3)(1) + √(2/3)(0)|² = (1/√3)² = 1/3
⟨0|0⟩ = 1 (normalization), ⟨0|1⟩ = 0 (orthogonality) — those two facts are doing all the work, collapsing the sum down to a single surviving term.
P(1) = |⟨1|ψ⟩|² = 2/3 (by the same method — or just 1 − 1/3, since they must sum to 1)

Worked example 2 — X measurement, with a phase twist

Let |ψ⟩ = (1/√2)(|0⟩ − |1⟩) — this is exactly the state |−⟩ from the table above. Measure the probability of getting |+⟩.

P(+) = |⟨+|ψ⟩|²
= |(1/√2)(⟨0|+⟨1|) · (1/√2)(|0⟩−|1⟩)|²
= (1/2)|⟨0|0⟩ − ⟨0|1⟩ + ⟨1|0⟩ − ⟨1|1⟩|²
= (1/2)|1 − 0 + 0 − 1|² = (1/2)(0)² = 0
The minus sign in front of ⟨1|1⟩ is the whole story — without it, the two surviving terms (1 and 1) would add to 2, not cancel to 0. This is destructive interference happening inside an actual calculation, not just a metaphor: since |ψ⟩ literally is |−⟩, and |−⟩ is orthogonal to |+⟩, getting exactly zero here isn't a coincidence, it's confirmation the arithmetic matches what orthogonality already guarantees. P(−) must then be 1 — you'd measure |−⟩ with certainty.
1.6

The general single-qubit state

Every possible pure single-qubit state (see the notebook's pure-state note for what "pure" means) can be written with just two real parameters, θ and φ:

|ψ⟩ = cos(θ/2)|0⟩ + e^(iφ)sin(θ/2)|1⟩
θ ranges from 0 to π. φ ranges from 0 to 2π.

θ controls the population split — how much weight is on |0⟩ vs |1⟩:

P(0) = cos²(θ/2) P(1) = sin²(θ/2)
These automatically sum to 1 (a standard trig identity), so no separate normalization check is ever needed once a state is written this way.

φ is the relative phase — the e^(iφ) sitting only in front of the |1⟩ term. This is the number that was +1, −1, i, or −i for the four named states above; here it's generalized to any angle at all.

relative phase vs. global phase Multiplying the entire state |ψ⟩ by an overall factor like −1 (a global phase, in front of every term equally) changes nothing measurable — every probability, every expectation value, comes out identical. Only the phase sitting between the |0⟩ and |1⟩ terms — the relative phase φ — is physically real. This is precisely why the formula above has no phase factor in front of the |0⟩ term: it would be a global phase, and physically inert.
1.7

The Bloch sphere — plotting θ and φ as a point

θ = angle down from the |0⟩ pole. φ = angle around the equator from the x-axis reference direction.

Every normalized pure state sits on the surface of a unit sphere — the Bloch sphere. Its coordinates come straight from θ and φ:

Bloch vector = ( sinθ cosφ, sinθ sinφ, cosθ )
These are just ordinary spherical coordinates — the same formula used for latitude/longitude on a globe, with θ measured from the north pole instead of the equator.

This lecture's convention places |0⟩ at the north pole and |1⟩ at the south pole (θ = 0 gives cos(0)=1, sin(0)=0, so the state formula collapses to pure |0⟩ regardless of φ — the north pole has no defined longitude, same as the Earth's own north pole).

worth double-checking against the notebook The main notebook's own Bloch-sphere diagram (1.1) uses |e⟩/|g⟩ labels at the same two poles — same object, different label convention (|0⟩=|g⟩, |1⟩=|e⟩, per the notebook's own notation table).

1.7c

The angle-doubling trap

Easy to trip on, worth its own callout: |0⟩ and |1⟩ are orthogonal in Hilbert space — a 90° relationship, by definition of orthogonal. But on the Bloch sphere, they sit at the north and south poles: 180° apart.

why the factor of two exists The state formula uses θ/2, not θ, precisely to absorb this mismatch — θ (the Bloch-sphere angle, drawn in the diagram above) is always exactly twice the actual angle between two states in the underlying Hilbert space. Two states that look 180° apart on the sphere (opposite poles, or any two antipodal points) are the ones that are genuinely orthogonal; two states drawn 90° apart on the sphere surface are not orthogonal at all. It's a real, easy-to-miss trap when reading the geometry off the picture instead of the formula.
1.8

Measurement, visualized as a projection

This state sits closer to |0⟩ (short trip to the top pole) — a Z measurement will more often collapse it to |0⟩ than |1⟩, in exact proportion given by the Born rule.

Every measurement basis from 1.4 lines up with one axis of the sphere: a Z measurement projects onto the vertical axis (poles = |0⟩, |1⟩), an X measurement projects onto the horizontal axis where |+⟩/|−⟩ sit, a Y measurement projects onto the axis where |+i⟩/|−i⟩ sit.

Any state whose arrow leans toward one pole is, geometrically, "closer" to that pole — and the Born rule (1.5) makes that visual intuition exact: the closer the state sits to a given pole, the higher the probability of collapsing there when measured on that axis. A state sitting exactly on the equator (like any of the four X/Y-basis states) is equidistant from both Z poles — a 50/50 coin flip under a Z measurement, which matches P(0)=cos²(π/4)=1/2 from the θ=π/2 row in the table above.

the general rule, stated once Measuring along any axis always collapses the state to one of that axis's two poles — never anywhere else on the sphere. Which pole, and how likely each outcome is, is exactly what the state's position (via the Born rule) determines.
